# Two Ways to Write a Sentence

Every probabilistic model of text answers one structural question before it
answers any other: *in what order, and conditioned on what context, are tokens
decided?* For a decade the field gave a single answer, left to right, each
token conditioned on the tokens already written. That answer is
**autoregression** (AR), and it underwrites GPT, Llama, and essentially every
deployed language model. It is so dominant that its central assumption is easy
to mistake for a law of nature rather than a modelling choice.

This site is about the other answer. **Diffusion language models** (dLLMs)
generate a sequence by starting from a fully corrupted draft, a row of mask
placeholders, and iteratively denoising it into clean text, revising many
positions in parallel and in no fixed order. Below is a real one doing exactly
that: a small model trained for this tutorial, every frame an actual decode
rather than an animation. Watch which positions it fills first, and notice
that they are nowhere near left-to-right:

## The champion, taken seriously

To understand what diffusion changes, first be precise about what it
challenges. Autoregression factorizes the joint distribution by the chain
rule in one fixed order:

$$
p(\mathbf{x}) = \prod_{i=1}^{L} p\left(x_i \mid x_{<i}\right),
$$

and this identity is *exact*: no approximation is made, which is why AR models
optimize true maximum likelihood and report exact perplexities. Three
engineering consequences follow, and each matters later.

**Training is parallel even though generation is not.** With ground-truth text
available, all $L$ conditional predictions can be computed in one forward pass
of a causally masked Transformer (teacher forcing). AR training saturates
accelerators; only *decoding* is sequential.

**The KV cache makes decoding cheap.** Causal attention means a new token
never changes the representations of earlier tokens. Their attention keys and
values can be computed once and cached, so generating token $i+1$ costs
attention over $i$ cached entries rather than a full recompute. Sequential,
but each step is light. Hold onto this: the KV cache is the single hardest
thing for diffusion to replace, and chapters 5 and 7 are largely about buying
it back.

**Exposure bias is baked in.** The model trains on ground-truth prefixes but
consumes its own imperfect samples at inference. Errors compound along a
single committed path, with no mechanism to revisit an earlier token.

## Four structural commitments

None of this is broken. But the left-to-right factorization carries four
commitments, and each is a place where an alternative paradigm can buy
something:

1. **Sequential decoding.** Producing $L$ tokens costs $L$ sequential passes;
   latency is lower-bounded by sequence length no matter how much hardware
   parallelism is available. A 1024-token answer takes 1024 round trips
   through the network.
2. **Unidirectional context.** Token $i$ sees only $x_{<i}$. The model never
   revises an early token in light of a later one, which is structurally
   awkward for infilling a hole in existing code, editing a document, or
   satisfying a constraint like "end the sentence with *frost*". AR systems
   need dedicated fill-in-the-middle training even to approximate this, and
   the gap still conditions only on a rearranged "left" context.
3. **Exposure bias.** The compounding-error problem above: one committed
   path, no revision.
4. **A fixed generation order.** Left to right is one ordering of a joint
   distribution, and not obviously the right one for code, math, or any
   output where some tokens are far easier to decide once others are fixed.
   Ask yourself where you would start writing a sonnet whose last word is
   fixed; it is probably not the first word.

<Callout kind="intuition">
Think of AR as writing with a pen that cannot be lifted: each word is final
the instant it is written, and you must commit to word $i$ before you may
think about word $i{+}1$. Diffusion is writing in pencil on the whole line at
once: sketch every position roughly, sharpen the ones you are most confident
about, erase and redo the rest. Pencil-and-revise can use the whole page as
context and fill it in any order, but each sharpening pass costs a full read
of the page. That single sentence contains most of the trade-offs in this
tutorial.
</Callout>

## One design space, two points

Here is the reframe that organizes everything on this site. Autoregression can
itself be described as a *corruption* process: take clean text and truncate
it, hiding everything after position $i$. Training then teaches the model to
predict clean tokens given the corrupted (truncated) view. Diffusion does the
same thing with a different corruption: hide a *random subset* of positions
rather than a suffix.

<Callout kind="keyidea">
**Generative sequence modelling is repeatedly predicting clean tokens from a
corrupted context.** Autoregression and diffusion are two points in one design
space. They differ only in (i) the **corruption process** that defines what "a
partially observed sequence" means, truncation versus masking, and (ii) the
**decoding order** in which positions are committed, fixed left-to-right
versus adaptive and parallel. Every capability difference in this tutorial,
parallel decoding, bidirectional context, self-correction, and also the
likelihood gap and the cache problem, is a consequence of those two choices.
Chapter 5 exhibits the model that dials continuously between the two points.
</Callout>

The word "diffusion" earns its place because the masking corruption is applied
*gradually*: a noise level $t$ runs from 0 (clean text) to 1 (all masks), and
the model learns to reverse the corruption from every intermediate level. That
is structurally the same recipe that generates images from Gaussian noise, and
chapter 2 shows the text version is actually *simpler*: no Gaussians, no score
functions, just masks and cross-entropy.

## The scoreboard, as of July 2026

What has this alternative actually delivered?

**Scale parity, at least at 8B.** LLaDA trained a pure masked-diffusion 8B
model from scratch on 2.3T tokens and reports it competitive with strong
similarly sized AR models such as Llama 3 8B on in-context learning
benchmarks<Ref k="nie2025large" />. Dream 7B matched that starting from
Qwen2.5 weights at a fraction of the cost<Ref k="ye2025dream" />. These are
existence proofs, not dominance: nobody claims diffusion beats the best AR
model at matched compute on general language modelling.

**Structural wins where bidirectionality matters.** A masked dLLM handles the
"reversal curse" (completing *B to A* after training on *A to B*) that trips
much larger left-to-right models<Ref k="nie2024scaling" />, and infilling is
native: mask the gap, run the denoiser, both sides are context automatically.

**Commercial speed.** Mercury reports 1,109 tokens per second for its Coder
Mini model on H100 in independent evaluations<Ref k="labs2025mercury" />; Seed
Diffusion reports 2,146 tokens per second on H20<Ref k="song2025seed" />;
Gemini Diffusion is Google DeepMind's experimental entry<Ref k="deepmind2025gemini" />.
Chapter 5 dissects what these systems actually are.

**The honest costs.** A dLLM reports a likelihood *bound*, not an exact
number. Each denoising step is a full forward pass over all positions, and
the exact KV cache does not transfer. The tooling, serving stacks, and
post-training recipes are years younger than AR's. Chapters 4, 6, and 7
quantify each of these.

<Callout kind="caveat">
The honest framing, maintained throughout this site, is a trade-off surface
rather than a winner. The scaling claims above are recent and mostly
single-source; the mathematics is stable, the empirical frontier is not.
Where a number matters we cite its exact source, and where it is a bound
rather than a measurement we mark it. When you see a bare "diffusion is
faster" or "diffusion matches AR" claim anywhere, your first question should
be: at what operating point, measured how?
</Callout>

## How to read this site

Chapters build in order, but three shortcuts work. The **fast path** to
understanding a modern dLLM is chapters 1, 2, 4, and 6: the anchor, the
objective, the decoder, and the taxonomy. **Practitioners** deciding what to
deploy should add chapters 5 and 7: the block dial and the
scaling/serving/post-training economics. **Safety and evaluation people** can
jump from chapter 2 directly to chapters 8 and 9. Every chapter closes with
pointers into the long-form PDF companion, which carries the full derivations
and 26 exercises.
