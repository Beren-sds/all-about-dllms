# Two Ways to Write a Sentence

Every probabilistic model of text answers one structural question before it
answers any other: *in what order, and conditioned on what context, are tokens
decided?* For a decade the field gave a single answer, left to right, each
token conditioned on the tokens already written. That answer is
**autoregression** (AR), and it underwrites GPT, Llama, and essentially every
deployed language model.

This site is about the other answer. **Diffusion language models** (dLLMs)
generate a sequence by starting from a fully corrupted draft, a row of mask
placeholders, and iteratively denoising it into clean text, revising many
positions in parallel and in no fixed order. Below is a real one doing exactly
that. It is a small model trained for this tutorial, and every frame is an
actual decode, not an animation:

<Callout kind="keyidea">
The organizing idea of everything that follows: **generative sequence
modelling is repeatedly predicting clean tokens from a corrupted context.**
Autoregression and diffusion are two points in one design space. They differ
only in (i) the **corruption process** that defines what "a partially observed
sequence" means, and (ii) the **decoding order** in which positions are
committed. AR corrupts by truncation (everything after position $i$ is hidden)
and decodes strictly left to right. Diffusion corrupts by masking (a random
subset of positions is hidden) and decodes in any order, many positions per
step.
</Callout>

## Why look past autoregression at all?

Autoregression is not broken. But the left-to-right factorization

$$
p(\mathbf{x}) = \prod_{i=1}^{L} p\left(x_i \mid x_{<i}\right)
$$

carries four structural commitments, and each is a place where an alternative
paradigm can buy something:

1. **Sequential decoding.** Producing $L$ tokens costs $L$ sequential forward
   passes. Latency is lower-bounded by sequence length no matter how much
   hardware parallelism is available.
2. **Unidirectional context.** Token $i$ sees only $x_{<i}$. Infilling,
   editing, and constraint satisfaction ("end the sentence with *frost*") are
   structurally awkward.
3. **Exposure bias.** Trained on ground-truth prefixes, the model consumes its
   own samples at inference; errors compound along a single committed path.
4. **A fixed generation order.** Left to right is one ordering of a joint
   distribution, and not obviously the best one for code, math, or structured
   documents.

<Callout kind="intuition">
Think of AR as writing with a pen that cannot be lifted: each word is final
the instant it is written. Diffusion is writing in pencil on the whole line at
once: sketch every position roughly, sharpen the positions you are most
confident about, erase and redo the rest. Pencil-and-revise can use the whole
page as context and fill it in any order, but each sharpening pass costs a
full read of the page.
</Callout>

## The scoreboard, as of July 2026

Diffusion is not a free lunch. It pays in likelihood (a dLLM reports a bound,
not an exact number), in sampling compute (each denoising step is a full
forward pass), and in engineering maturity (the KV cache that makes AR
inference cheap does not transfer cleanly). What it buys: parallel decoding,
bidirectional context, native infilling, and revisable generation. At the
7&ndash;8B scale, pure masked-diffusion models such as LLaDA<Ref k="nie2025large" />
and Dream<Ref k="ye2025dream" /> are reported competitive with similarly sized
AR models, and commercial systems<Ref k="labs2025mercury" /><Ref k="song2025seed" />
serve diffusion-style decoding at over 1,000 tokens per second.

<Callout kind="caveat">
The honest framing, which this site maintains throughout, is a trade-off
surface rather than a winner. The scaling claims above are recent and mostly
single-source; the mathematics is stable, the empirical frontier is not. Where
a number matters, we cite its exact source, and where it is a bound rather
than a measurement, we say so.
</Callout>
