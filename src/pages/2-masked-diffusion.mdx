# How Masked Diffusion Works

Image diffusion corrupts with Gaussian noise, but tokens have no "a little bit
noisy": a token is either the right one or a different one. Discrete diffusion
therefore corrupts by *replacement*, and this chapter builds the variant that
conquered language. By the end you will have the full modern objective, the
architecture that trains it, an honest account of what it costs, and the exact
mathematical relationship to autoregression. None of it requires more than the
chain rule and a logarithm.

## Choosing the corruption: why the mask won

D3PM, the founding paper of discrete diffusion, framed corruption as a Markov
transition matrix applied per token per step, and studied a whole family of
kernels<Ref k="austin2021structured" />. Two members matter for language:

- **Uniform kernel**: with some probability, replace the token with a random
  vocabulary token. Corrupted text looks like text with typos: plausible but
  wrong tokens, silently scattered.
- **Absorbing kernel**: with some probability, replace the token with a
  dedicated mask symbol `[M]`. Once masked, always masked (the mask
  "absorbs"), and real tokens never turn into other real tokens.

The absorbing kernel has a property that turned out to dominate everything
else: **corruption is observable**. The model can see exactly which positions
are damaged (they display `[M]`) and which are trustworthy. Under the uniform
kernel the model must first *detect* the damage before repairing it, and the
training signal mixes detection errors with prediction errors. Observable
corruption is the leading explanation for why absorbing/masking diffusion
outperforms uniform diffusion in practice, though not the only hypothesis:
masking also inherits BERT-shaped infrastructure, and the benchmarks that
crown winners emphasize likelihood, where masking's tighter bound shines.
Chapter 6 returns to what the uniform kernel gets in exchange (revision that
lives in the state space itself, and a Gaussian dual that imports few-step
samplers<Ref k="sahoo2025duo" />).

## The forward process, precisely

Fix a noise level $t \in [0,1]$. The forward process takes clean text
$\mathbf{x}_0$ and masks each position independently with probability $t$
(this is the linear schedule; general schedules replace $t$ by a masking
probability $1-\alpha_t$):

$$
q\left(x_t^i \mid x_0^i\right) =
\begin{cases}
x_0^i & \text{with probability } 1-t \\
\texttt{[M]} & \text{with probability } t
\end{cases}
$$

At $t=0$ the text is clean; at $t=1$ it is a row of masks. A single sequence
yields training examples at every corruption level, from "one blank to fill"
to "reconstruct everything from nothing".

## The objective: weighted fill-in-the-blanks

Diffusion training classically starts from a variational bound (an ELBO) with
a ladder of per-step KL terms. For the masking kernel, the entire apparatus
collapses<Ref k="sahoo2024simple" /><Ref k="shi2024simplified" />. The
negative ELBO becomes

$$
\mathcal{L} \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{t}\; w(t)\;
\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\!\sum_{i:\ x_t^i = \texttt{[M]}}
-\log p_\theta\!\left(x_0^i \mid \mathbf{x}_t\right),
\qquad w(t) = \tfrac{1}{t} \text{ for the linear schedule.}
$$

Read it aloud: *mask some tokens, predict the originals, weight by noise
level*. It is BERT's training signal upgraded to a principled generative
model, with the mask-ratio curriculum and the weight $w(t)$ derived rather
than guessed. Minimizing it maximizes a lower bound on the data
log-likelihood, so the model is a real generative model with a real (if
bounded) likelihood, not a heuristic infiller.

<details class="derivation">
<summary>Why the ELBO collapses (sketch)</summary>

Two facts do the work. (1) Under the absorbing kernel, the per-step posterior
$q(x_{t-1}^i \mid x_t^i, x_0^i)$ is nontrivial only at masked positions, and
there it either stays masked or reveals the true token, so each KL term
reduces to a cross-entropy against the clean token. (2) Summing per-step terms
over a finer and finer time grid turns the sum into an integral over $t$ whose
integrand is the masked cross-entropy weighted by the schedule's rate,
$w(t) = -\alpha_t' / (1-\alpha_t)$. Substituting the linear schedule
$\alpha_t = 1-t$ gives $w(t) = 1/t$. The full derivation, every step, is
Appendix F of the PDF companion.
</details>

## A worked example, seven characters

Take `T H E _ C A T` with positions 4 and 6 masked at $t = 0.5$, so
$w = 2$. The model sees `T H E [M] C [M] T` and must place probability on the
originals at the masked slots. Suppose it assigns the true tokens
probabilities $0.5$ and $0.25$. This draw contributes

$$
w \cdot \left(-\log 0.5 - \log 0.25\right) = 2 \times (0.693 + 1.386) \approx 4.16 \text{ nats.}
$$

That is the entire loss computation. No noise vectors, no score functions, no
KL tables, and note what the model exploited: the masked position 4 is
constrained by context on *both* sides. A left-to-right model predicting
position 4 would see only `T H E`.

## The architecture underneath: a Transformer minus one mask

Nothing in the objective specifies a network. In practice the denoiser
$p_\theta(x_0^i \mid \mathbf{x}_t)$ of every modern dLLM is an almost
unmodified Transformer, and the deltas from GPT are worth listing exactly,
because they explain why the paradigm could scale so fast:

- **Delete the causal mask.** Every position attends to every other, since
  inferring a masked token needs both left and right context. This one change
  is simultaneously the source of the bidirectionality advantage *and* of the
  KV-cache problem: masklessness buys context and forfeits caching.
- **Keep the vocabulary head.** The output is a softmax over the vocabulary
  at every position, identical to an AR model's head. There is no separate
  "noise head" and no regression target.
- **Drop time conditioning entirely.** Continuous diffusion injects the noise
  level through embeddings and adaptive normalization. For masked diffusion,
  a remarkable result says the optimal denoiser is **time-independent**: the
  mask pattern already encodes the noise level<Ref k="ou2024absorbing" />.
  Several dLLMs therefore condition on nothing but the partially masked
  sequence.

The practical upshot is large: a dLLM reuses AR pretraining infrastructure,
kernels, and even weights (chapter 6's adapted family) almost verbatim,
swapping only the attention mask and the loss. The paradigm shift lives in the
objective and the decoder, not the network.

## The deepest fact: any-order autoregression

Because time is removable, the objective can be rewritten with no reference to
noise levels at all, and what appears is exact and surprising:

$$
\mathcal{L} \;=\;
\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\sum_{i=1}^{L}
-\log p_\theta\!\left(x_0^{\sigma(i)} \mid x_0^{\sigma(<i)}\right),
$$

an average of autoregressive losses over *uniformly random generation orders*
$\sigma$<Ref k="ou2024absorbing" /><Ref k="shi2024simplified" />.

<Callout kind="keyidea">
**AR is masked diffusion restricted to one order, and masked diffusion is AR
averaged over all $L!$ orders.** Everything else follows from this single
difference. Fixing the order recovers the exact chain rule, hence exact
maximum likelihood and the best perplexity, but forfeits parallelism and
revision. Averaging over orders buys any-direction context and
many-tokens-per-step, but a single network must now fit every order with
finite capacity, so its per-order fit is looser, and its reported
"perplexity" is an upper bound computed from the ELBO rather than an exact
number. When you see a dLLM perplexity next to an AR perplexity, you are
seeing a bound next to a measurement; honest tables mark the difference.
</Callout>

Two distinct forces separate the paradigms on likelihood, and it pays to name
them: **averaging versus specializing** (one network spread over all orders
versus specialized to one) and **bound versus exact** (ELBO versus true NLL).
Both shrink with scale and better schedules; neither vanishes. The flip side
is that the very thing that costs likelihood, training on all orders, is what
enables parallel decoding, bidirectional context, native infilling, and the
reversal-curse win, none of which a single-order objective can provide.

## Training it at scale: three practical choices

Three implementation decisions separate a toy from an 8B dLLM, each a direct
consequence of the objective:

1. **Estimator variance.** The loss is a Monte Carlo estimate of an integral
   over $t$, and $w(t) = 1/t$ *diverges* as $t \to 0$. Naive uniform sampling
   of $t$ yields rare, enormous gradient contributions from lightly masked
   draws. Scale controls this with stratified (low-discrepancy) noise-level
   sampling and related variance reduction. This is not a footnote: when we
   trained the 1.85M-parameter model behind this site's widgets, the naive
   estimator stalled training at the unigram level for thousands of steps,
   and the diagnosis chain is written up in the PDF's appendix. The same
   variance problem returns at higher stakes in preference optimization and
   RL (chapter 7).
2. **Instruction tuning by partial masking.** Supervised fine-tuning keeps
   the prompt clean and masks only the response, so the model learns
   $p_\theta(\text{response} \mid \text{prompt})$ under the same objective
   used in pretraining<Ref k="nie2025large" />. No architectural change, just
   a restriction of which positions may be masked.
3. **Compute per step.** Bidirectional attention with no cache means every
   step is a full pass over all positions. Pretraining cost is comparable to
   AR (both do full passes anyway); the asymmetry is baked in at *inference*,
   which is chapter 4's subject.

<Callout kind="caveat">
Three caveats worth carrying forward. The "why masking won" story above is the
leading explanation, not a theorem, and chapter 6 gives the competing
hypotheses their own hearing. The any-order freedom is a fact about
*training*; chapter 4 shows deployed dLLMs exercise surprisingly little of it.
And the ELBO-versus-NLL gap means every likelihood comparison in the
literature needs a footnote; chapter 7's evaluation rules make that footnote
mandatory.
</Callout>
