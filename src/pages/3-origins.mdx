# Where dLLMs Came From

The intellectual history of dLLMs is usually told as "image diffusion, then
text diffusion." That telling skips the most interesting part: text generation
attacked the autoregressive bottleneck *before* diffusion arrived, invented
most of the decoder, and then stalled for lack of a theory.

<Timeline />

## The prehistory: everything but the warrant (2018-2019)

Non-autoregressive machine translation<Ref k="gu2018nonautoregressive" />
generated every output token simultaneously in one pass, an order of magnitude
faster, and immediately hit the wall it named the **multimodality problem**:
sampling positions independently mixes fragments of incompatible answers. That
failure still governs every parallel decoder built since; it is what you see
at NFE 1 in this site's playground.

Iteration was the obvious fix, and the pieces landed fast. Lee et
al.<Ref k="lee2018deterministic" /> framed iterative refinement explicitly as
learning a *conditional denoising autoencoder*, the score connection written
down for text in 2018. Then Mask-Predict<Ref k="ghazvininejad2019maskpredict" />
assembled what is recognizably the modern dLLM decoder: train a masked
language model on the target, decode by predicting all positions in parallel,
then repeatedly re-mask and re-predict the tokens the model is *least
confident* about, for a constant number of iterations. All-mask start,
parallel prediction, confidence-ranked commitment, fixed step budget: 2019.

In the same year, XLNet<Ref k="yang2019xlnet" /> trained on expected
likelihood over *all permutations of the factorization order* (the any-order
objective, used for representation learning rather than generation), Wang and
Cho sampled text from BERT itself<Ref k="wang2019bert" />, and the Insertion
and Levenshtein Transformers<Ref k="stern2019insertion" /><Ref k="gu2019levenshtein" />
generated text through insert and delete operations, reaching a length-$n$
sequence in $\lfloor \log_2 n \rfloor + 1$ insertion rounds.

<Callout kind="keyidea">
By the end of 2019, text generation had parallel prediction, confidence-based
re-masking, any-order objectives, and edit-based decoding, everything a modern
masked dLLM **does**. What it did not have was a reason to believe any of it:
no likelihood bound, no principled mask schedule, no account of what the
iteration converges to. Diffusion's contribution to text was the *warrant*:
D3PM gave the masked LM a generative model and an ELBO<Ref k="austin2021structured" />,
the masked NELBO gave the mask-ratio curriculum a derivation<Ref k="sahoo2024simple" />,
and the any-order identity gave XLNet's objective a decoder<Ref k="ou2024absorbing" />.
</Callout>

## The diffusion road (2020-2024)

The continuous machinery arrived with DDPM<Ref k="ho2020denoising" />, crossed
to discrete data with D3PM's transition-matrix formulation<Ref k="austin2021structured" />,
was pushed to GPT-2-level perplexity by score entropy (SEDD)<Ref k="lou2023discrete" />,
and finally collapsed to the weighted cross-entropy of chapter 2 by the masked
diffusion papers<Ref k="sahoo2024simple" /><Ref k="shi2024simplified" />. A
parallel continuous-state route (Diffusion-LM's embeddings, SSD-LM's
simplex<Ref k="li2022diffusionlm" /><Ref k="han2022ssdlm" />) pioneered
gradient-based controllability and block decoding, and survives today in the
niches where those matter.

## The other road: flow matching (2022-)

Just as masked diffusion consolidated, a second formalism re-derived it from
different primitives. Flow matching<Ref k="lipman2023flow" /> replaces "invert
a fixed corruption" with "**choose** a path from noise to data, then regress
its velocity"; diffusion paths are one choice, straight paths a better one in
continuous spaces<Ref k="liu2023rectified" />. On discrete state spaces the
velocity becomes the rate matrix of a continuous-time Markov chain, and the
resulting Discrete Flow Models provably *contain* discrete diffusion as a
special case<Ref k="campbell2024generative" />. Choosing the source
distribution to be a point mass on `[M]` recovers exactly the masked model of
chapter 2, learned posterior time-independence included<Ref k="gat2024discrete" />.

| | Continuous FM | Discrete FM | Masked diffusion |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Velocity" | vector field | CTMC rate matrix | reverse posterior (fixed) |
| Path | any noise-to-data | any source, mixture paths | absorbing marginal (fixed) |
| Trained object | velocity net | posterior $p_\theta(x_1^i \mid \mathbf{x}_t)$ | denoiser $p_\theta(x_0^i \mid \mathbf{x}_t)$ |
| Loss | velocity regression | weighted cross-entropy | weighted cross-entropy |
| Sampler freedom | solver choice | rate-matrix choice, post hoc | unmasking order only |

Two independently developed formalisms arriving at the same trained object for
the mask path is the strongest evidence that the masked dLLM is a natural
point in design space rather than a historical accident. And the flow view is
already paying rent: edit operations returned with a proper generative
semantics (Edit Flows<Ref k="havasi2025edit" />, closing the loop to the
Levenshtein Transformer), and kinetic-optimality analysis explains why
mixture/mask-like paths keep winning<Ref k="shaul2025flow" />.

<Callout kind="caveat">
This tidy narrative takes a side in a genuinely contested credit assignment.
The MT prehistory worked on *conditional* generation where multimodality is
mild; whether its recipes could have scaled to open-ended language modelling
without diffusion's schedule theory and likelihood story is untestable. And
formalism containment is not benchmark dominance: as of 2026 the strongest
trained artifacts still sit at the mask corner, with the largest published
discrete-flow-matching LM at 1.7B parameters<Ref k="gat2024discrete" />.
</Callout>
