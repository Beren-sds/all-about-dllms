# The Decoding Playground

Diffusion's promised advantage, emit many tokens per step instead of one, is
realized or squandered entirely at decoding time. This chapter's widgets run
on a real model: a 1.85M-parameter character-level masked dLLM trained on
Shakespeare specifically for this tutorial, with an identical autoregressive
twin as the referee. Small enough to train on a laptop, real enough that every
curve below is a measurement.

## The knob: number of function evaluations

An AR model generating $L$ tokens performs exactly $L$ sequential passes, not
negotiable. A masked dLLM performs $N$ denoising steps, and $N$ is a **free
parameter**: each step may commit many positions at once. $N = L$ recovers
one-token-per-step; $N \ll L$ trades quality for a large latency win. Try it:

<DecodingPlayground client:visible />

Three commitment policies are available above, and they are not equal:

- **Random order**: unmask a random subset each step, following the schedule.
  This is what the raw theory samples, and it wastes the model's own
  information.
- **Confidence order**: commit the positions where the model's prediction is
  most peaked, leaving uncertain positions for later steps, when more context
  exists. "Easy tokens first" is the move AR structurally cannot make.
- **Noisy confidence**: confidence plus annealed random noise
  (MaskGIT-style), guarding against the rich-get-richer failure where early
  confident commitments (whitespace, common tokens) drag the whole canvas
  toward degenerate text.

## The measured trade

<NfeExplorer client:visible />

Three lessons, all visible in the data. First, **NFE 1 is the multimodality
problem in one number**: every position sampled independently gives generative
perplexity in the thousands. Second, quality improves monotonically with
steps, and confidence-based ordering beats random by roughly 7x at high NFE.
Third, the latency panel shows the honest serving picture: this cache-free
dLLM undercuts its AR twin's sequential sampling only below a crossover NFE
(128 on this hardware), because each diffusion step is a full pass over all
positions while the AR twin re-reads only one new token per step. Chapter 7
turns that observation into arithmetic.

<Callout kind="keyidea">
Decoding a dLLM is a two-dimensional choice: **how many steps** (the
compute-quality dial) and **in what order** (the policy). Neither exists for
AR. Deployed dLLMs sit at an adaptive interior point: they commit a handful of
confident tokens per step, behaving semi-autoregressively in practice, so the
theoretical any-order freedom is mostly held in reserve rather than exercised.
</Callout>

## Revision: the move AR cannot make

A position committed early can be **re-masked** if later commitments make it
look wrong, then re-predicted. LLaDA uses low-confidence remasking at
inference<Ref k="nie2025large" />; no AR analogue exists, since AR's pen
cannot be lifted. Remasking is also, as chapter 8 argues, the one genuinely
new *defensive* affordance dLLMs bring to safety.

<Callout kind="caveat">
A decoding pathology can be a training pathology in the sampler's clothes.
While building this site's model, an early undertrained checkpoint collapsed
to near-blank text under confidence ordering, and quality *worsened* with more
steps; the healthy retrained model shows none of this. If your dLLM decodes
badly, diagnose the model before redesigning the decoder. The judge-gaming
toggle above carries the matching evaluation warning: generative perplexity
alone rewards low-entropy samplers, so it must never be reported without a
diversity measure.
</Callout>
