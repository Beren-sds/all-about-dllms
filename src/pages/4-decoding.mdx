# The Decoding Playground

Diffusion's promised advantage, emit many tokens per step instead of one, is
realized or squandered entirely at decoding time. Training gives you a
denoiser; *how you call it* determines speed, quality, and most of the
failure modes. This chapter's widgets run on a real model: a 1.85M-parameter
character-level masked dLLM trained on Shakespeare for this tutorial, with an
identical autoregressive twin (same architecture, same data, same steps,
different attention mask and loss) as the referee. Small enough to train on a
laptop; real enough that every curve below is a measurement, not a sketch.

## The first dial: number of function evaluations

An AR model generating $L$ tokens performs exactly $L$ sequential passes; not
negotiable. A masked dLLM performs $N$ denoising steps, the **number of
function evaluations** (NFE), and $N$ is a free parameter decoupled from $L$,
because each step may commit many positions at once. $N = L$ recovers
one-token-per-step (no speedup, best quality); $N \ll L$ commits several
tokens per step in parallel (large speedup, some quality loss). The central
inference problem of the whole paradigm is choosing where to sit on that
curve. Try it yourself; drag the NFE control and watch how the same model
becomes a different animal:

## The second dial: which positions to commit

Given a step budget, which masked positions get filled this step? The theory's
default is uninformative, and the improvements are where practice lives:

- **Random order** unmasks a random subset each step, following the schedule.
  This is what the raw ELBO samples, and it wastes the model's own
  information: at any step, some masked positions have sharply peaked
  predictive distributions (the model is sure) and others are flat (it is
  guessing).
- **Confidence order** commits the positions with the most peaked
  predictions, leaving uncertain ones for later steps, when more context has
  been filled in. "Easy tokens first" is a move AR structurally cannot make,
  because its order is fixed. On the measured curves below, confidence
  ordering beats random by roughly 7x in generative perplexity at high NFE.
- **Noisy confidence** adds annealed random noise to the confidence scores
  (MaskGIT-style) before ranking. The guard matters because pure confidence
  ordering has a rich-get-richer failure: early confident commitments (in
  our character model, whitespace) reshape the canvas so that similar tokens
  become the confident choice everywhere, and the decode collapses toward
  degenerate text. We met this failure honestly while building the site, of
  which more below.
- **Semi-autoregressive block schedules** commit left-to-right in chunks,
  running diffusion within each chunk. This decoding *policy* on a
  full-canvas model is one of three distinct things "block diffusion" can
  mean, and teasing them apart is chapter 5's job.

And the move none of them have: **remasking**. A position committed early can
be re-masked if later commitments make it look wrong, then re-predicted.
LLaDA ships low-confidence remasking at inference<Ref k="nie2025large" />; no
AR analogue exists, since AR's pen cannot be lifted. Remasking is also, as
chapter 9 argues, the one genuinely new *defensive* affordance dLLMs bring to
safety.

## The measured trade

<NfeExplorer client:visible />

Read the two panels together and three lessons fall out.

**NFE 1 is the multimodality problem in one number.** Every position sampled
independently in a single pass gives generative perplexity in the thousands:
chapter 3's 2018 failure, reproduced on demand. Coherence is bought with
iterations because each step conditions on the joint structure the previous
steps committed.

**Policy dominates at every budget.** The gap between confidence-guided and
random ordering is not a refinement; it is multiples. Decoding research is
not garnish on dLLMs, it is where much of the quality lives.

**The latency panel is the honest serving picture.** This cache-free dLLM
undercuts its AR twin's sequential sampling only below a crossover NFE (128
on this hardware), because each diffusion step is a full pass over all
positions while the cached AR step touches one new token. "Diffusion is
parallel, therefore faster" is not automatic; it is an engineering claim that
holds at the right operating point. Chapter 7 turns this into arithmetic and
surveys the cache retrofits that move the crossover.

<Callout kind="keyidea">
Decoding a dLLM is a two-dimensional choice AR never gets to make: **how many
steps** (the compute-quality dial) and **in what order** (the policy).
Everything on this page generalizes: production systems tune both dials, add
confidence thresholds that adapt the number of commitments per step, and
increasingly restore block structure for caching. One humbling empirical
note: at deployment, confidence-based decoding mostly commits tokens adjacent
to already-revealed context, so deployed dLLMs behave semi-autoregressively,
and the theoretical any-order freedom is largely held in reserve.
</Callout>

## A debugging story you should steal

While building this site's model, our first checkpoint produced a paradox:
under confidence ordering, quality got *worse* as NFE increased, the opposite
of everything above. The canvas collapsed to near-blank text, and more steps
meant deeper collapse. It would have been easy to blame the sampler and start
tuning it. The actual cause was upstream: the checkpoint had stalled at the
unigram level during training (a variance pathology of the $1/t$-weighted
objective at small batch sizes; chapter 2), so "most confident" meant
"whitespace, everywhere". The healthy retrained model shows textbook behavior
under the *same* sampler.

<Callout kind="caveat">
A decoding pathology can be a training pathology wearing the sampler's
clothes: diagnose the model before redesigning the decoder. And carry the
matching evaluation warning, visible in the widget's toggle above: the AR
judge scores its *own* samples (2.8) as more probable than genuine held-out
Shakespeare (5.5). Generative perplexity rewards low-entropy samplers, so it
must never be reported without a diversity measure. Chapter 7 promotes both
warnings into a checklist.
</Callout>
