# Making dLLMs Useful

A pretrained base model is not a product. Four practical questions stand
between the objective of chapter 2 and something you would deploy: does the
paradigm scale, can it be aligned and taught to reason, can it be served, and
how do you even compare it fairly against the incumbent. Each gets a section,
and each section has at least one number that circulates in garbled form,
corrected here.

## Scaling: same exponent, constant tax, and a data crossover

The first scaling law fitted for masked diffusion models finds a scaling
*rate* comparable to autoregressive models with a "relatively small compute
gap", a constant-factor tax paid for training on all
orders<Ref k="nie2024scaling" />. Chapter 2 derived where the tax comes from
(averaging versus specializing, bound versus exact); the scaling study shows
it does not worsen with scale. The same work shows what the tax buys: their
1.1B masked model breaks the reversal curse that defeats much larger AR
models, 13B Llama-2 and 175B GPT-3 included, and unsupervised classifier-free
guidance improves conditional generation essentially for free.

Now the correction. The **16x figure from this paper is not the scaling-law
gap**, though it is routinely quoted as one. It is a trade-off statement about
generation: a masked model given 16x the pretraining compute of an AR baseline
matches its performance while sampling 1.4x *faster* than the
KV-cache-accelerated ARM. The scaling-law gap itself is reported only
qualitatively as small. Quoting "diffusion needs 16x the compute" as a
general law misstates both claims.

The data axis flips the comparison. In data-constrained settings, masked
diffusion "significantly outperform[s] AR models when compute is abundant but
data is scarce"<Ref k="prabhudesai2025diffusion" />: random masking implicitly
trains over many token orderings, acting as an implicit data augmentation
that keeps extracting signal from *repeated epochs* long after AR saturates,
and the paper derives a closed-form critical compute threshold where
diffusion overtakes AR for a given unique-token budget.

<Callout kind="keyidea">
Read the two laws together as a phase diagram in the (compute, unique data)
plane. Along the compute axis at abundant data, AR wins by a constant factor
that scale does not erase. Along the data axis at abundant compute, there is
a computable crossover beyond which diffusion wins, because every repetition
of a sequence presents it under new masks while AR re-reads it in the same
order. The field's largest models are marching toward exactly the regime,
data-bound and compute-rich, where the diffusion side of the diagram grows.
That, more than benchmark parity, is the structural argument that dLLMs are
not a curiosity.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="caveat">
Both studies are single-team and fitted at roughly 1B-parameter, academic
token budgets, two orders of magnitude below frontier runs; whether the
exponents and the closed-form crossover extrapolate is untested, and neither
covers block dLLMs, whose hybrid objective plausibly sits between the fitted
curves. The phase-diagram reading is an extrapolation, labeled as such.
</Callout>

## Post-training: rebuilding a stack around a missing number

The AR post-training stack, SFT then preference optimization then RL, leans
at every stage on one convenience: an exact, cheap
$\log p_\theta(\mathbf{y} \mid \mathbf{x})$ per completion, computed in one
teacher-forced pass. A dLLM does not have that number; it has the ELBO, an
*expectation over mask patterns and noise levels*, so every log-probability a
post-training algorithm requests becomes a Monte Carlo estimate with
variance. Each stage of the stack has been rebuilt around that missing
number:

- **SFT survives unchanged**, because it never needed the exact likelihood:
  mask only the response, keep the prompt clean, train the same weighted
  cross-entropy (chapter 2).
- **Preference optimization** compares log-probabilities of preferred and
  rejected responses, so replacing each with a noisy bound injects variance
  directly into the preference gradient. LLaDA 1.5's variance-reduced
  preference optimization budgets the Monte Carlo samples so the *difference*
  of bounds driving the gradient is stable<Ref k="zhu2025llada" />.
- **RL for reasoning** arrived in 2025 with two recipes, both, at bottom,
  variance-and-cost dodges. diffu-GRPO (the d1 recipe) integrates
  critic-free policy gradients into masked dLLMs using an efficient
  *one-step* estimate of log-probabilities, scoring the completion with a
  single unmasking pass, plus a fresh random masking of the prompt at every
  policy-gradient update so no single mask pattern's estimation error is
  systematically reinforced<Ref k="zhao2025d1" />. DiffuCoder's coupled-GRPO
  instead evaluates completions under *complementary* mask draws, an
  antithetic-sampling move that covers every token across the pair, worth
  +4.4% on EvalPlus for their 7B code model<Ref k="gong2025diffucoder" />.
- **Reasoning natively**: Diffusion-of-Thoughts adapts chain-of-thought so
  the whole reasoning trace sharpens jointly across denoising iterations
  rather than being emitted token by token, trading compute for reasoning
  quality through the NFE knob<Ref k="ye2024diffusion" />. The structural
  argument for diffusion-native reasoning is revision: an AR chain carries an
  early arithmetic slip forward, while a denoiser can re-mask and repair an
  earlier step when later steps contradict it. Whether that argument survives
  contact with practice is genuinely open; the RL results show dLLM reasoning
  *improves* with RL, not that it beats matched AR models.

<Callout kind="keyidea">
Post-training a dLLM is one problem wearing three costumes: the exact
sequence log-probability that SFT never needed, preference optimization needs
as a difference, and RL needs per update, exists only as a noisy bound. Every
2025 method is a point on the bias-variance-cost triangle: VRPO buys low
variance with more samples, diffu-GRPO buys cheapness with one-step bias plus
mask randomization, coupled-GRPO buys variance reduction with antithetic
masks. None of this machinery would exist if the model reported an exact
likelihood, which is the single deepest consequence of averaging over
generation orders.
</Callout>

## Serving: the cache taxonomy and the break-even arithmetic

Chapter 4 measured the problem; here is the design space. Every dLLM cache
relaxes AR's "provably unchanged" to something weaker, and the schemes sort
cleanly by where they pay:

| Scheme | Exactness | Cost paid where | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| AR KV cache | exact | native | causal attention freezes prefixes |
| BD3-LM<Ref k="arriola2025block" /> | exact across blocks | training (block-causal mask) | finished blocks are frozen context |
| Eso-LM<Ref k="sahoo2026esoteric" /> | exact | training (restructured computation) | AR-masked fusion with cacheable phases |
| dKV-Cache<Ref k="ma2025dkv" /> | approximate (delayed) | none (training-free) | cache K/V one step after a token decodes; 2-10x |
| Fast-dLLM<Ref k="wu2025fastdllm" /> | approximate (block-stale) | none (training-free) | reuse K/V outside the active block; with adaptive parallel decoding, up to 27.6x |

Beneath every cache sits the error source chapter 3 named: tokens committed
in the same step are sampled independently. The mitigations map onto where
they intervene: **decode-time** (confidence thresholds committing only peaked
predictions, Fast-dLLM's adaptive version), **train-time** (EDLM's
sequence-level energy correction, chapter 6), and **sampler-time**
(DiffuCoder's finding that sampling temperature changes not just token
choices but the *generation order itself*<Ref k="gong2025diffucoder" />, an
order-diversity knob AR serving has no analogue of).

The whole question compresses into arithmetic. Generating $L$ tokens costs
cached AR roughly $L^2/2$ in attention reads; a cache-free dLLM at $N$ steps

## Six rules for comparing without fooling yourself

The two paradigms report different quantities under the same names, so
paradigm comparisons have a distinctive failure taxonomy. Each rule below
names where it stops applying, because a checklist without scope conditions
is how these pitfalls arose in the first place.

1. **Pin the operating point, or report the curve.** A dLLM result is a
   point on an (NFE, quality) curve; a comparison at one unstated NFE is
   unfalsifiable. *Skip when* comparing two fixed production systems, where
   the shipped configuration is the object of study.
2. **Annotate bound versus exact.** Diffusion "perplexity" is an ELBO upper
   bound with estimator noise; AR perplexity is exact. Print the difference.
   *Skip when* both sides use the same estimator.
3. **Match compute, not steps.** An AR step is one cached token; a diffusion
   step is a full uncached pass. Compare at matched FLOPs per generated token
   or matched wall-clock, and say which. *Skip when* the claim is explicitly
   about step counts at fixed model.
4. **Never report generative perplexity alone.** It is minimized by
   low-entropy degenerate samplers; this site's own judge scores its own
   samples above real Shakespeare (chapter 4). Pair it with a diversity
   measure. *Skip when* quality is task accuracy with verifiable answers.
5. **Hold data and tokenizer fixed.** Adaptation provenance adds a confound:
   an adapted dLLM carries its AR parent's corpus. State provenance; for
   scaling claims use matched data. *Skip when* the question is explicitly
   best-system-versus-best-system.
6. **Separate the paradigm from the serving stack.** Commercial throughput
   composes model, cache scheme, and proprietary engineering; attribute speed
   to the measured configuration, not to "diffusion". *Skip when* the source
   itself scopes the claim to the system.

<Callout kind="caveat">
Everything in the post-training section is 2025-vintage, single-team, and
evaluated on small-to-mid benchmarks; none of it has the replication depth of
the AR RLHF stack it imitates. Two named open risks: one-step
log-probability estimates are biased and no published analysis bounds how the
bias steers the learned policy; and reward hacking through the *decoder*, a
policy learning to exploit confidence-based unmasking order, appears entirely
unstudied. And the six rules encode 2025-26 failure modes; agentic and
long-horizon evaluation of dLLMs has no protocol yet.
</Callout>
