# Safety When Decoding Is Parallel

Alignment, as deployed, is a property of left-to-right generation more than
its practitioners tend to state. A refusal is a **prefix**: the model decides
early, emits "I can't help with that," and every later token conditions on
having refused. Moderation pipelines assume the same geometry, scoring a
response as it streams and cutting it off early. A masked dLLM voids both
assumptions at once: there is no privileged early position where refusal
happens, and there is no stream, because many positions commit per step.

## The mask-infilling attack, step by step

The first dedicated attack, DIJA<Ref k="wen2025devil" />, constructs
*interleaved mask-text prompts*: the harmful request is partially written out,
with the dangerous specifics left as literal mask tokens for the model to
fill.

1. **The prompt arrives as a scaffold.** The model is not asked to *begin* a
   response (where refusals live); it is asked to complete gaps inside text
   that already exists.
2. **Bidirectional consistency does the work.** In the authors' analysis,
   "bidirectional modeling drives the model to produce contextually consistent
   outputs for masked spans, even when harmful". The same infilling pressure
   that makes a dLLM a great code editor presses it to complete a harmful
   scaffold coherently.
3. **Parallel commitment removes the checkpoint.** "Parallel decoding limits
   model dynamic filtering and rejection sampling of unsafe content": by the
   time any monitor could react, the span exists on the canvas.

The reported numbers are not marginal: up to 100% keyword-based attack success
on Dream-Instruct, and gains over the strongest prior jailbreak baseline of up
to 78.5 percentage points in evaluator-based success on JailbreakBench.

## Why AR-inherited alignment transfers poorly

Nothing in the standard pipeline targets this surface. SFT refusals are
learned as response-initial patterns, and an infilling query never asks for a
response beginning. Preference optimization on a dLLM already fights estimator
variance before any safety-specific difficulty enters (chapter 7). Streaming
moderation has no purchase on a canvas where the harmful span materializes
mid-sequence at step 3 of 32. In the atlas's terms: alignment techniques were
fitted to one decoding policy, sequential and prefix-first, and are being
deployed on machines whose policy axis moved.

<Callout kind="keyidea">
**Every dLLM capability has a safety dual.** Bidirectional infilling (the code
feature) is scaffold-completion pressure (the attack). Parallel decoding (the
latency feature) removes sequential refusal checkpoints (the defense that no
longer works). Revision (the quality feature) is the one genuinely new
defensive affordance: a canvas-level monitor can in principle re-mask a
harmful span before finalization, an option AR streaming never had. Safety
work that treats a dLLM as "an LLM with a different sampler" will keep being
surprised.
</Callout>

## Defense directions, honestly labeled

Published defenses are thin as of 2026, so directions rather than results:
canvas-level moderation scored per denoising step rather than per streamed
token; infilling-aware alignment that includes interleaved mask-text formats
in safety tuning so refusal becomes a property of completions, not prefixes;
and decode-time monitors reading the model's own confidence and
unmasking-order dynamics, signals with no AR analogue. None is validated at
scale in public work.

<Callout kind="caveat">
This chapter leans on a young literature: one flagship attack paper, its own
metrics (keyword-based success flatters attacks; evaluator-based numbers run
lower), and no published at-scale defenses to weigh against it. Commercial
systems disclose little about their safety stacks, so the public picture may
understate deployed defenses. What is robust is the structural analysis: the
assumptions alignment inherited from AR decoding demonstrably do not all
survive the move to parallel generation.
</Callout>
