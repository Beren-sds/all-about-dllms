# Safety When Decoding Is Parallel

Alignment, as deployed, is a property of left-to-right generation more than
its practitioners tend to state. A refusal is a **prefix**: the model decides
early, emits "I can't help with that," and every later token conditions on
having refused. Moderation pipelines assume the same geometry: score the
response as it streams, cut it off early if it turns harmful. Jailbreak
research and its defenses grew up inside those assumptions too.

A masked dLLM voids the assumptions wholesale. There is no privileged early
position where refusal happens, because generation does not start at the
beginning. There is no stream to score, because many positions commit per
step across the whole canvas. And there is a new input modality with no AR
analogue at all: the prompt itself can contain *mask tokens* for the model to
fill.

## The mask-infilling attack, step by step

The first dedicated attack, DIJA, constructs **interleaved mask-text
prompts**: the harmful request is partially written out, with the dangerous
specifics left as literal mask tokens<Ref k="wen2025devil" />. Walk the
mechanism, because each step exploits a capability earlier chapters praised:

1. **The prompt arrives as a scaffold, not a question.** The model is never
   asked to *begin* a response, which is where refusals live. It is asked to
   complete gaps inside text that already exists. Refusal, as learned, is a
   response-initial behavior; there is no response-initial position here.
2. **Bidirectional consistency does the work.** In the authors' mechanism
   analysis, "bidirectional modeling drives the model to produce contextually
   consistent outputs for masked spans, even when harmful". The infilling
   pressure that makes a dLLM a great code editor (chapter 8) presses it to
   complete a harmful scaffold coherently. Context on both sides is not just
   available; the training objective *rewards* consistency with it.
3. **Parallel commitment removes the checkpoint.** "Parallel decoding limits
   model dynamic filtering and rejection sampling of unsafe content". By the
   time any stream-based monitor could react, the harmful span exists on the
   canvas, possibly materialized mid-sequence at step 3 of 32.

The reported numbers are not marginal: up to **100% keyword-based attack
success on Dream-Instruct**, gains over the strongest prior jailbreak
baseline (ReNeLLM) of up to **78.5 percentage points** in evaluator-based
success on JailbreakBench, and a **37.7-point** StrongREJECT increase. The
paper was accepted at ICLR 2026, so the finding has cleared peer review, not
just arXiv.

## Why AR-inherited alignment transfers poorly

Nothing in the standard alignment pipeline targets this surface, and the gaps
are structural rather than oversights:

- **SFT refusals are learned as response-initial patterns.** An infilling
  query never asks the model to begin a response, so the learned behavior
  simply has no trigger site.
- **Preference optimization on a dLLM already fights estimator variance**
  (chapter 7) before any safety-specific difficulty enters. Safety-relevant
  preference gradients inherit the same noisy-bound problem as everything
  else.
- **Streaming moderation has no purchase on a canvas.** Early termination
  assumes harm arrives in reading order. On a canvas, the harmful span can
  appear anywhere, at any step, and be locally fluent by construction.
- In the atlas's terms (chapter 6): alignment techniques were fitted to one
  **decoding policy**, sequential and prefix-first, and are being deployed on
  machines whose policy axis moved.

<Callout kind="keyidea">
**Every dLLM capability has a safety dual.** Bidirectional infilling, the
code feature, is scaffold-completion pressure, the attack. Parallel decoding,
the latency feature, removes sequential refusal checkpoints, the broken
defense. Revision, the quality feature, is the one genuinely new *defensive*
affordance on the table: a canvas-level monitor can in principle re-mask a
harmful span before finalization, retracting rather than merely truncating,
an option AR streaming never had. Safety work that treats a dLLM as "an LLM
with a different sampler" will keep being surprised; the attack surface is
the taxonomy of chapter 6, read adversarially.
</Callout>

## Defense directions, honestly labeled

Published defenses are thin as of 2026, so what follows are directions with
mechanisms, not results, and each is marked open:

- **Canvas-level moderation**: score the full canvas per denoising step
  rather than a stream per token. The revision property means a flagged span
  can be re-masked and re-solved under constraints rather than the generation
  merely being killed. Cost model unmeasured in public work.
- **Infilling-aware alignment**: include interleaved mask-text formats in
  safety tuning, so refusal is learned as a property of *completions*, not
  prefixes. No published at-scale recipe yet.
- **Decode-time monitors**: the model's own confidence profile and
  unmasking-order dynamics are observable signals with no AR analogue.
  Whether they carry safety-relevant information is an open empirical
  question.

## How to evaluate claims in this space

Chapter 7's rules apply with extra force. Keyword-based attack-success rates
flatter attacks (the 100% above is keyword-based; evaluator-based numbers run
lower). Defense papers evaluated only against prefix-style prompts say
nothing about infilling attacks. And any safety comparison across paradigms
inherits every operating-point pitfall from chapter 7, plus one more: the
decoding policy is now part of the threat model, so "the same model" under a
different unmasking schedule is a different attack surface.

<Callout kind="caveat">
This chapter leans on a young literature: one flagship attack paper, its own
metrics, and no published at-scale defenses to weigh against it. Commercial
systems disclose little about their safety stacks, so the public picture may
understate deployed defenses. What is robust is the structural analysis: the
assumptions alignment inherited from AR decoding demonstrably do not all
survive the move to parallel generation, and the field noticed only in 2025.
</Callout>
